# Kovzhr

> A festival of wax antlers begins when the city's thirteenth shadow refuses its owner.

## The Procession Without a Calendar

The festival has no appointed day. It begins when thirteen people cross the eastern square and fourteen shadows remain behind them. The extra shadow stands beside the fountain until the bell keeper notices that it casts a little light onto the stones.

He then covers the town bell with red cloth. The streets are swept toward their centers. Every household places a bowl of warm wax beside its door, and by evening small antlers have formed above each surface.

The people wear those antlers without fastening them. The wax remembers the shape of each head and remains cool until the procession reaches the square.

No one speaks their own name during the festival. Names are written on scraps of white silk and tied to the legs of chairs that are left outside overnight.

## The Chair Maker's Exception

A chair maker discovered that his thirteenth chair had no place to tie a name. Each leg became smooth when touched by silk. Each knot slid down until it rested on the ground.

He carried the chair to the square. Beside the fountain, the extra shadow sat down before he had lowered it. The chair became heavy, and a line of warm wax appeared across its seat.

Within the wax he saw an image of his workshop: the same benches, the same unfinished wood, but one wall missing. Beyond that wall stretched a field of enormous bones arranged like fallen trees.

He recognized a piece of wood among them. It was the curved back of the thirteenth chair, which he had found beside a road and brought home without wondering what had bent it.

The bell keeper covered the chair with his own red cloth. The town bell began ringing from somewhere beneath the square.

## The Wax That Held a Footprint

The procession stopped. Across the people's antlers moved little flames, though the wax did not melt. Their light showed a second square beneath the paving stones.

In that lower square, fourteen chairs surrounded a fountain full of dark leaves. Thirteen held shadows. The final chair held a small living tree whose branches were shaped exactly like the wax antlers above.

The chair maker placed his hand on the covered seat. Through the red cloth he felt a footprint, small and warm. He remembered finding a child's shoe beneath his workbench on the morning after bringing home the curved wood.

He had left the shoe outside, expecting its owner to return. By noon it had become a piece of dark bark, and he had burned it with the workshop's other scraps.

## The Sleeper Beneath the Festival

The bones beyond the missing wall belonged to a dragon that had never finished becoming a tree. Its antlers held the city's shadows. Its roots were roads along which people carried wood, water, and things they did not recognize until too late.

Once each turning of its sleep, a shadow escaped to the square. The festival kept that shadow warm enough to remember its owner, while the names tied to chairs prevented it from entering the wrong house.

The thirteenth chair had been carved from a living part of the creature. Its shadow had not escaped. It had followed its own wood back into the city, bringing a small footprint that needed a place to stand.

The bell keeper lowered his head. His wax antlers had grown branches. From one branch hung a little shoe made of red cloth.

## Four Cuts Beneath the Seat

The chair maker turned the chair carefully. Along its underside appeared four marks:

*Kov — zhr — ul — aesh.*

He spoke them toward the fountain. The dark leaves in the lower square rose through the stones and settled around the extra shadow's feet. The footprint beneath the cloth became deeper.

The chair maker asked for a bowl of unused wax. He pressed the little bark-shaped memory of the shoe into it, although his hand held nothing anyone else could see.

A pair of wax feet appeared. Above them grew the outline of a child-sized tree, its branches carrying fourteen small shadows. One by one, those shadows left the branches and returned beneath the people standing in the square.

The covered bell became silent. The chair lost its weight.

## The Street After the Red Cloth

At dawn, every name was untied from its chair. The silk strips were burned in household bowls, leaving a little ash shaped like a leaf. The wax antlers softened and slipped from the people's heads without touching the ground.

The chair maker returned to his workshop. Its missing wall stood whole. Beneath the bench lay a child's shoe, ordinary and worn along one edge. He placed it on the windowsill instead of outside.

The thirteenth chair remained beside the fountain. During the day it held only light. At night, a little tree could be seen in its shadow, standing with its roots planted firmly on the stones.

The festival has no appointed day.

The bell keeper watches the eastern square. The households keep their bowls ready.

And beneath the city, a dragon with unfinished branches sleeps around the warmth of a footprint that no longer has to follow its own wood home.
